Outside Corsica Studios last year, waiting for The Hellp to go on, a London TikToker called Emma Winder started filming the queue. Skinny jeans, pointed boots, a studded belt, a leather jacket worn like armour against a crowd, not the cold. She called the look a "Hedi Boy" after Hedi Slimane, and posted two videos: *What is the current Hedi Boy fit today?* and *The Hedi Boy UK Invasion*. Between them they did the numbers a scene usually takes years to earn. A year on, the look is still working its way through Shoreditch and Dalston.

Slimane earned the reference the hard way, and earlier than most of the people wearing it now were born. His Dior Homme, from 2000 to 2007, cut menswear down to something almost skeletal and photographed it on musicians rather than models. He shot Pete Doherty around London for most of a decade and collected the results into *London: Birth of a Cult*, a 2005 book of a city he clearly preferred in black and white. The clothes, the pictures, and the idea that the two were the same thing travelled together. He left Celine, his last house, in October 2024, and has not taken another post since — which makes a trend named after him, two years into his absence from the industry, a strange kind of tribute.

Stranger still once you look closely at what the Hedi Boys are actually wearing. Highsnobiety, covering the same queues, noted the thing the coverage kept circling without quite saying outright: almost none of it is Slimane's own design. The skinny jeans aren't Dior Homme. The leather jacket is whatever the nearest shop had. What's being reproduced is the photograph, not the product — the grain, the thinness, the sense of a boy lit like a rock star under a streetlamp. Twenty years on, London is dressing from an image of itself rather than from a label.

That is, in its way, exactly the move Slimane made first. His own photographs weren't documentary so much as a decision about what London was allowed to look like: monochrome, underslept, a little too thin, lit for drama rather than comfort. He left out the colour and the comfort because the lens he was working in had no use for them. The Hedi Boys outside Corsica Studios are running the same exclusion in reverse, editing a decade they didn't live through down to its most photographable parts, with the soundtrack — The Hellp, Snow Strippers, 2Hollis, all leaning on the same electroclash sources Slimane's crowd once did — doing the work of making the borrowed years feel current rather than costumed.

None of which makes it a costume exactly. A look that survives its own designer, outlives the clothes it was supposedly about, and gets reassembled by people working from photographs rather than archives, isn't nostalgia in the usual sense — nostalgia remembers. This is closer to translation: a generation building the image back up from a description of it, getting some of it wrong, and producing something that is, at a certain distance and in the right light, indistinguishable from the thing it was copying. Slimane's camera used to decide what London got to keep. For now, without him in the room, London is deciding that for itself.
